Why the Moon Turns Red During a Total Lunar Eclipse

2026-11-24 · AI-assisted, human-reviewed before publication

During a total lunar eclipse, Earth’s shadow covers the Moon as it moves into the shadow’s inner region, called the umbra. The Moon grows dim, but it does not produce a new color of its own: sunlight that passes through Earth’s atmosphere still reaches the lunar surface. That altered illumination can make the Moon look orange or red. NASA describes the Moon’s surface as largely covered by charcoal-gray dust and rocky debris, called lunar regolith; during an eclipse, it is the light reaching that surface that changes its appearance. [1] [2]

The color comes from how Earth’s atmosphere filters sunlight. Sunlight contains different colors, and shorter-wavelength blue and violet light scatter more easily in the atmosphere than longer-wavelength red and orange light. During an eclipse, sunlight that has passed through a thick slice of atmosphere reaches the Moon; more of the red and orange light makes it through while much of the blue and violet has scattered away. The Moon’s reddish appearance is therefore caused by the filtered sunlight illuminating it, rather than by a change to the Moon’s surface. [1]

The shade is not always the same. NASA notes that the Moon can appear redder when there is more dust or cloud in Earth’s atmosphere during the eclipse. The same kind of filtering helps explain the colors of sunrise and sunset: sunlight travels through a longer path in the atmosphere, and more of its blue light scatters away before the remaining yellow-to-red light reaches an observer. In a lunar eclipse, filtered sunlight reaches the Moon instead. NASA compares the effect to the world’s sunrises and sunsets being projected onto the lunar surface. [1]
