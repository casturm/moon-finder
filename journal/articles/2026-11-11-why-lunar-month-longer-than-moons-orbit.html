Why a Lunar Month Is Longer Than the Moon’s Orbit

2026-11-11 · AI-assisted, human-reviewed before publication

The Moon takes about 27.3 days to complete one orbit around Earth, but about 29.5 days to go from one new moon to the next. Those intervals describe different cycles. The first measures the Moon’s revolution around Earth; the second measures the return of the same Sun-Earth-Moon arrangement, which determines the phase we see. NASA describes the phase cycle as repeating about every 29.5 days and gives the Moon’s orbital period as 27.322 days. [1]

The difference is that Earth does not stand still while the Moon orbits. Earth travels around the Sun, so the direction from Earth to the Sun shifts during the Moon’s orbit. After the Moon has made one revolution, it has returned to roughly the same place in its orbit around Earth, but the Sun-Earth-Moon alignment has changed. The Moon therefore needs to keep moving along its orbit to reach the position that restores the same phase. [2] [1]

A simple way to picture it is to imagine walking one lap around someone while that person moves around a larger circle. Completing your lap does not necessarily put you in the same direction relative to a fixed point beyond the larger circle; you need to go a little farther. In this case, the relevant reference is the Sun: phases depend on how much of the Moon’s sunlit half is visible from Earth. A full moon occurs when the Moon is opposite the Sun as seen from Earth, while at new moon the Moon is in the same part of the sky as the Sun. [1]

That extra travel accounts for the roughly 2.2-day difference between the two periods. The 27.3-day orbit is not a complete phase cycle; it measures a revolution around Earth. The longer, 29.5-day interval is the time needed for the Moon’s changing position to bring the Sun, Earth, and Moon back to a comparable alignment—and for the familiar sequence of phases to repeat.
