Why the Moon’s Far Side Is Not Its Dark Side

2026-11-21 · AI-assisted, human-reviewed before publication

The Moon’s “far side” is the hemisphere we generally cannot see from Earth—not a hemisphere that is always dark. The Moon is tidally locked to Earth: it rotates once on its axis during each orbit around our planet, so the same side usually faces us. That matched rotation and orbit create the familiar near side and far side, defined by our viewpoint, not by how much sunlight each receives. [1] [2]

Sunlight still reaches both hemispheres over time. The Moon’s day side faces the Sun, while its night side faces away; as the Moon rotates and travels around Earth, those regions change. The far side therefore has lunar daytime and nighttime, just as the near side does. From Earth, we see the changing phases because the Moon’s position relative to the Sun changes how much of its sunlit half is visible to us. At full Moon, the side facing Earth is illuminated. At new Moon, that near side is in darkness while the far side is sunlit. [1] [2]

“Dark side” can also be confused with places that truly receive little or no direct sunlight. Near the lunar poles, some craters contain permanently shadowed regions. Those localized areas are distinct from the far side as a whole: they are defined by persistent shadow, whereas “far side” means the hemisphere turned away from Earth. NASA reports high concentrations of water ice in these polar shadowed regions, but that does not mean the entire far hemisphere is permanently dark. The Moon’s near and far sides both experience changing illumination; permanently shadowed polar terrain is a separate feature of the lunar surface. [1] [2]
