Why the Moon Is Sometimes Visible During the Day

2026-10-26 · AI-assisted, human-reviewed before publication

The Moon is not only a nighttime sight. It can be above the horizon while the Sun is up, and sometimes appears as a faint, pale presence in the daytime sky. It does not make its own light: sunlight illuminates half of the Moon, and the portion of that lit half visible from Earth changes as the Moon orbits us. Daytime visibility therefore depends in part on the phase. The Moon’s position and visibility also vary with the time of year and your location on Earth, so it is not visible every day or equally easy to spot everywhere. [1]

Near new Moon, spotting it is especially difficult. The Moon is in roughly the same part of the sky as the Sun, and its sunlit side faces away from Earth while its dark side faces us. It rises and sets with the Sun, but is not visible in the daytime sky in this phase. As the Moon moves into a waxing crescent, a thin sunlit curve becomes visible; it rises in the morning after sunrise and sets at night after sunset. The crescent grows as more of the Moon’s dayside comes into view. [1]

The Moon’s rise and set times continue to shift through the cycle. Around first quarter, it rises near midday and sets around the middle of the night, making it high in the sky during the evening. A waxing gibbous Moon rises in the afternoon and sets in the early morning. Full Moon rises around sunset and sets around sunrise. After that, a waning gibbous Moon rises after sunset and sets in the morning; last quarter rises around the middle of the night and sets around midday. A waning crescent rises before sunrise and sets in the afternoon. These are approximate patterns for most latitudes; consult local predictions for exact times. [1]
