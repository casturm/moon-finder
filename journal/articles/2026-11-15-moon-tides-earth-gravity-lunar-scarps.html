The Moon Has Tides Too—But They Don’t Look Like Earth’s

2026-11-15 · AI-assisted, human-reviewed before publication

On Earth, tides are easiest to notice as ocean water rising and falling along shorelines. But gravity distorts both worlds: the Moon’s pull slightly changes Earth’s shape, and Earth’s gravity slightly deforms the Moon. Earth is about 80 times as massive as the Moon, yet the lunar response is far less dramatic than ocean tides. Instead of a conspicuous surge of water, the effect is a small change in the solid lunar surface. [1]

That change is small but measurable. Astronomers use lasers bounced off the lunar surface or special reflectors placed there to make extremely precise measurements. Earth’s gravitational tide can change the Moon’s surface by about 4–6 inches (10–15 centimeters). As the surface moves up and down, the points reflecting the laser light move with it. The gravitational effect therefore has to be accounted for in these measurements. [1]

Earth’s pull may also contribute to features on the Moon called lobate scarps: young cliffs that look like steps in the landscape. The Moon’s hot interior cools, and the resulting contraction pushes the crust together. In some places, the crust buckles, breaks, and is pushed upward, forming a cliff. NASA describes scarp formation as involving both this contraction and Earth’s gravitational pull on the lunar surface. [1]

Images from NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter have revealed thousands of these scarps. Their orientations provide evidence that Earth’s gravity contributes to the formation of the cracks. That evidence does not make Earth’s pull the only factor: the Moon’s contraction as its interior cools is also part of the process described by NASA. Together, laser measurements and scarp patterns show two ways the gravitational interaction can be detected—through the Moon’s slight flexing and, potentially, in the stresses involved in shaping its surface.
